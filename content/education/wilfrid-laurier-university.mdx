Faculty of Science, Waterloo, Ontario. Graduated with a GPA of 8.41 / 12
overall — 9.8 in Computer Science, 8.6 in Psychology.

I couldn't choose between computer science and psychology, so I took both. 
Computer science gave me access to deep understanding of computers, logic, 
data structs, programming, and my favorite - theoretical computation.
I started off hating mathematics, being unable to sit through lectures and ended
up really enjoying discrete maths and proofs. Before agentic coding I spent so many hours
cursing compilers and segfaults, understanding how to solve complex problems by drawing on napkins. 
Diagrams, analogies, going mad over cryptic errors. It wasn't glamorous but it was such a difficult
and well-rewarded challenge because I can know understand very technical subjects quickly. Some of my standout
coursework is using C to simulate operating systems - deadlocks, scheduling, I/O. Implementing algorithms like
DFS/BFS/A* for navigation, google result scraping + classification using basic ML.
Agentic coding has changed so much, but it hasn't removed the spirit of what we do as developers. Is it stockholm syndrome?
I do miss the absolute high of fixing something so specific to the code when there was no StackOverflow articles
to help you, but I do love the way that time is reclaimed. The problem space has moved and AI is not perfect so there
is always a reward to working as a developer, it's just changed.

Psychology is so surface level, until it isn't... Pop psychology is so wrought with
misinformation and "cringe" for lack of a better word - people think they can be master
manipulators and get what they want by doing "dark psychology" tricks off tiktoks narrated by AI voices.
Once I started to get into deeper topics - especially neuroscience, I started to really
enjoy the work I was doing. I specialized into perceptions and neuroscience, and
it really broadened how I view (haha) the world of computers. The brain *is* a computer and
the [interface theory of perception](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26384988/) really blew my mind.
What psychology offered was a different perspective of science, the writing and abstract thinking.
There was no formulas or correct answers; I wrote papers to propose research, and wrote papers on research
I did. The most fascinating example of that is the research we did on mice - one behaviour and another biological study.
Caffienating rats and observing 20 minutes of video to count exactly how many times they crossed quadrants
and groomed themselves. We also marijuanated mice, euthanised, their brains extracted and chilled.
Grad students handled the not-nice parts, but I handled slicing the brain into nanometer-thick slices and then
then observing them under the microscope for changes in brain activity under the effects of marijuana.

I find the whole combination of computers and brains so interesting and ultimately the goal
I set out to achieve after graduating was to get lots of skilled experience, working
in team environments, and actually delivering things that mean something. I would like to work in
fields that combine the two passions, and especially if the deliverables provide real good to the world.
Computers and brains share a lot of things, and there's always something needed that combines the two.
