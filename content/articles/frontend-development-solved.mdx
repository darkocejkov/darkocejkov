i was just laid off.
company not doing hot, seo is dead, blah.

it's quite hard to see through the fog right now about the real landscape of what development looks like,
especially frontend development. it has me wondering about if frontend development as a specialized skill is needed
anymore. unless you get real complex with it, the regular company no longer needs frontend developers to make buttons do the thing.
AI does it, faster, better, and without needing benefits. sure i know how to prompt AI towards the goal better than
a non-technical stakeholder but i'm sure they end up at the same goal, without having to pay me AND my tokens.

i knew it was over when my product owner had his own charts made, that was the writing on the wall.
thankfully i had the job of productionizing the code and tying it into real data, but it was basically done.
if i had tickets to make a new dashboard, it took a day to get the code done (with agents). reusable components
is why, and i no longer have to worry about my WPM or forgetting something, agents execute pretty much flawlessly.
suddenly, the difficult task of having to organize these complex systems to get dynamic forms rendering was trivial
because we had done it well before, and now the agents can reuse that structure.

with an AI mandate, i basically proved to the higher-ups that someone can wield a harness well enough to do 5 developers
worth of work in a day. i kept delivering, pushing myself, trying to cram tickets in that weren't even approved yet.
i worked fast and loose because i could tell that we were so close to the end of what was the "lift and shift".
i wanted to be the one to champion the last few pieces, the last few things that could get our "beta" app into the real thing.
the victory i wanted was just a sprint away, this marathon was almost over. i started the project, and i wanted to be the one
to finish it. yet again, another victory pulled out under my feet. this time not because of lack of knowledge. 
a cascading kaleidoscopic crashing of everything, the economy (ew), the popularization of ai (killing seo), and whatever
other shenanigans are going on.

despite not "finishing" in the way i thought, i at least accomplished *something* that i can show off, and while SEO
was never an interest of mine (sorry david/tylor, i remember being enthusiastic about SEO in the interview, i really don't care for it lol),
it was interesting to learn about. ironic that it's pretty much dead now, but at least it was there. most importantly, it really wasn't
about the product in the end because the product already existed, it was about learning all the things i did. and especially about the people.
it's hard to believe that i never had met anyone working at moz that i didn't like: everyone is intelligent, honest, kind, and delivers their best. also surprisingly queer too.
sitting here in my dim office desk, there's an empty spot, the laptop now peripheral to me instead of the focus.

the routine is gone, the people are gone. it was never meant to last forever but i'm grateful for it. 

but god i wish i could skip the recruiting part.
work is fun, fulfilling, precise, exact, it's measureable, there's people to talk to, things to learn.
job hunting is the most exhausting, unfulfilling, antisocial, totally imperfect, unforgiving, torturous process.
there is zero transparency, hundreds of applications through the worst systems (upload your resume here! thanks! nevermind that, here's a form to fill out every resume detail you have that is difficult to use and there's no formatting options). 

"Tips for Resume Building To Get You Hired At FAANG: 1. ONE PAGE RESUMES ONLY 2. You can have as many pages on your resume you want! :)"

The entire process is meant to wear you down, there's a dozen interviews each an hour+ long, there's no feedback (if a company does give you feedback, it's vague). The typical rules for applying to jobs are so arbitrary and there's ZERO way to tell if a strategy you used worked, unless it lands you the job, but it won't work for other people. It's a right-place-right-time sort of thing. Because of this fact, I know it's best to just arrive professionally, be candid, be real, and be personable. There's no gaming the system (other than networks and connections). Applying to jobs and being hired on merit and personality alone is one of the hardest things to do. You rely entirely on the judgement of 
strangers on your skills and personality. Every rejection is painful because you wonder what to do differently, but if you're not playing a "character" the judgments chip away at your own self-reflection because that's what you show up as. yourself. 
I haven't even had the worst record of rejections and it still haunts me to be in this position. No one likes the hiring
process, and it's just gotten even harder because AI now exists.

Not only are you judged by some internal AI tool based on your resume submission and barely get to grace the eyeballs of a real person, the other candidates are streamlining code directly into their retinas while they sleep, they are the 10x developer that can deliver 10x the work AND understand it. at least, that's what it feels like. not a single developer really knows how good they are, there's always those ultra-achievers that know algorithms you'd never think of in O(1) for breakfast, they study cutting-edge papers in the bath, they read hackernews before bed, and dream of optimization. 

AI has widened that gap for me. I now feel like I don't know anything about frontend development because AI does it better than anyone does. instead digging into the details an agent can run parallel test environments, git bisect, trace thousands of function uses back to find a bug while i'm still reading the ticket. i've had agents that ran the whole test environment in parallel with the browser + playwright to find the bug. agents can read code so complex without caring, and i have seen agents look so deep into code that it starts looking at node_modules itself, it is able to view memory in realtime and do complex debugging operations. i'm still reading the ticket. With MCPs it's talking to datadog and sentry to locate a server error log, 1 of 10 million in an hour - while being able to parse and understand the format of the logs (which are DEFINITELY not uniform, they all have different shapes and properties, you don't know the name of the service that produces this). it cross references backend code and pinpoints the exact line causing the error. i've just started my IDE and went to get coffee.

when the project doesn't involve innovation or really pushing the boundaries of code, we were just implementing.
and AI took that away from us. I don't feel like my skillset truly matters anymore, because I haven't accomplished
outstanding development examples that sit on awwwards. what i mean to say is that the skillset i worked so hard on is now concretely installed as a skill with best practices in an agent, that can be shared as a markdown file. i'm good at prompting agents to do the work i did, but i don't need to exercise that anymore. in contrast to the developer that has specialized in something more abstract or challenging for AI, who is more sought out after. this person is made up, because there are specializes in which AI is not as helpful, but it's for sure great at writing code.

Technology has always been a means to an end. Am I so caught up in the fact that I no longer need to implement code myself? I miss the reward, the fact that i get to say that i solved something myself. and it's not advertised but AI is not a "thinking" general intelligence. it's an advanced statistical model that uses theories of learning/thinking to better take intermediate outputs and refine them to best answer the prompt. Since code is so syntactical, it's hard for me to remember that it guesses because 90% of its output is so good at what i ask it for. there's an obvious 5% that can be improved by not being lazy and prompting it better and providing more context, then that last 5% for what neither you nor the agent know. it's much more obvious in basic comfyUI AI image generation workflows, because it's visually wrong in some way, and adjusting the prompt becomes a game in fixing the problem but not introducing another. 
Code models have intense ways to include thinking, planning, and execution as well as skills.
